The SEBD Grand Tour 🍾

The SEBD grand tour, 1993–2026 — with provenance and a certificate.
From Bertram Ludäscher & two AI toolchains, the morning of the SEBD'26 closing talk.

AI prose Thirty-four editions ago, SEBD — founded by Domenico Saccà — began in Gizzeria Lido; this year it reached Senigallia, where the closing talk was about trustworthy AI results: answers that arrive with evidence, not just confidence. The morning of the talk, as a small self-referential demo, we asked: what is the shortest grand tour visiting every SEBD edition, from the first to the thirty-fourth? This page is the keepsake version of that demo — and, in the spirit of the talk, it does not ask you to believe it. Every number below carries the means to check it. Let it make you smile — and let you verify.

primary source The 34 editions and their host cities come from the DBLP index of SEBD proceedings (2026 Senigallia supplied by hand — DBLP doesn't have the future yet). Their coordinates come from OpenStreetMap/Nominatim, checked place by place on 2026-08-01 — see the coordinate audit for why that sentence used to read differently, and what it cost.

The page has two parts. Part I is the provenance: how the answer came about, from DBLP through two AI toolchains to a post-talk audit that found four wrong coordinates. Part II is the evidence: a tree of what the final claim actually rests on, and how to check each piece yourself. The two are related but not the same, and the difference is the point of the page.

The question (Q)

Visit all 34 SEBD editions exactly once, starting at edition 1 (Gizzeria Lido, 1993) and ending at edition 34 (Senigallia, 2026), minimizing total distance. Cities that hosted twice — Rimini, Venice, Villasimius — count once per edition, which is why everything downstream is keyed by edition number, not city name. modeling choice

The answer (A)

AI-generated, verified The optimal tour is 3,527 km — versus 15,209 km in chronological order (76.8% shorter; time travel is inefficient travel). The visiting order, by edition number:

1 29 21 25 24 15 26 19 11 16 12 32 28 33 22 3 23 8
27 10 4 30 17 7 13 5 31 9 20 2 18 6 14 34

modeling choice Honesty about the metric: distances are straight lines on an equirectangular km-plane (reference parallel 42°N, R = 6371 km), rounded to whole kilometers — not roads, not rail, and pointedly not ferries, though the Sicily and Sardinia editions cross open water. Because that projection is a genuine plane embedding, the underlying real-valued metric satisfies the triangle inequality exactly. The rounded matrix we actually ship inherits it only to within the rounding: 22 of the 35,904 ordered triples violate it, every one of them by a single kilometer. Nothing downstream cares — the certificate below is a claim about the shipped integer matrix, whatever its geometry — but a page about checkable claims should not round a number and keep the adjective. Should you actually take the trip, budget extra for the sea legs.

Almost all of it is one documented modeling choice, which is the outcome you want. But note what the old page reported: a gap of only 5.9 km (3,546.9 − 3,541), and it was smaller for a bad reason. Our coordinates were then wrong in a way that made the tour 14 km longer (3,541 instead of 3,527 on the same visiting order), which happened to cancel most of the 20 km projection difference. Corrected 2026-10-16: until then this paragraph said the wrong coordinates made the tour “12.3 km shorter” — the wrong direction, caught by the second review.

What turned "almost" into "provably" was neither narrator — it was the certificate (Part II): an exact solver whose lower bound climbs 2881 → 3273 → 3439 → 3475 → 3477 → 3527, meeting a feasible tour exactly there. Lower = upper = optimum.

The model didn't make this trustworthy. The workflow did — and the workflow only caught the data error because a second, disagreeing witness was kept in the room.

Until 2026-08-01 this page claimed its coordinates were "town centroids, accurate to a couple of km," and invited you to spot-check them against any map. Nobody had. When someone finally did — geocoding all 34 stops against OpenStreetMap/Nominatim — four were wrong, one of them badly:

The errors are not random, which is the interesting part. All four stops are coastal frazioni — a Lido, a Marina, a beach hamlet — and in every case the coordinate had drifted inland toward the parent comune that shares the name. That is exactly the failure mode you would predict of a language model recalling a place from its name rather than looking it up, and exactly what a gazetteer catches in one pass. What it cost: the certified optimum was 3,541 km and is now 3,527 km; the chronological comparison moved 15,245 → 15,209 km. What it did not cost: the visiting order is unchanged, and every structural claim on this page — the sandwich argument, the six cuts, the 16 co-optima — survived verbatim. A certificate proves the tour is shortest for the numbers it was given. It has nothing whatever to say about whether those numbers describe Italy. That is not a flaw in the method; it is the precise boundary of what the method claims, and this page spent two weeks demonstrating it by accident.

The GPT bundle, for its part, had Torre Canne and Squillace Lido right all along, and Castellaneta Marina wrong by 10.0 km and Gizzeria Lido by 3.5 km — so neither toolchain was trustworthy on coordinates, and neither was checkable without a third source. verified

An unresolved fact — where was SEBD 1998?

The edition table above, like both AI toolchains, follows DBLP: the 6th SEBD (1998) was in Ancona. On 2026-10-20 a third source turned up that disagrees. The symposium's own history page, written by a participant, says: “Nel 1998 siamo tornati al mare, a Portonovo, un posto di sogno (scogli e spiaggia) vicino ad Ancona” — in 1998 we went back to the sea, to Portonovo, a dream spot (rocks and beach) near Ancona. Portonovo also hosted the 14th SEBD in 2006. If 1998 was there too, editions 6 and 14 are a fourth twin pair, and the tour's input changes. primary source

Searching further did not settle it. The proceedings title says “Ancona”; the official events list says “Ancona, Marche” but links each edition to DBLP, so it repeats the same witness; papers cite the proceedings, not the venue; and an AI assistant (Gemini) offered that the meeting was “highly likely” on the Ancona engineering campus, without a source. Portonovo lies within the comune of Ancona, so “Ancona” may name the place at a coarser resolution.

Rather than pick a winner, we wrote the situation down as rules (venue1998.lp), keeping three kinds of statement apart:

  • Known (strict facts): what each source says; that the events list repeats DBLP and that the Gemini claim cites no source (so both are undercut); that Portonovo lies within the comune of Ancona.
  • Assumed (an explicit choice, both options left open): what “Ancona” on the title page refers to — the city where the meeting was held, as proceedings titles usually do, or only the comune, which includes Portonovo.
  • Defeasible: a reliable source saying X supports X, unless the conflicting venue holds.

An answer-set solver then reports every reading the evidence allows, and which assumption each one makes. The diagram is drawn from the rules, node by rule; it is a view of them, not a translation into an argumentation framework, which would lose the structure that says why each argument stands or falls.

How to read the runs, without answer-set programming.

An answer set is a consistent story. The solver lists every story that respects the rules. A story must contain all the known facts; pick one meaning of “Ancona”; conclude a venue only if a reliable source supports it and nothing in the same story defeats it; and contain nothing it cannot justify from the rules.

Why three stories. If “Ancona” names only the comune, the label says nothing about the city, so only the eyewitness speaks: Portonovo — one story. If it names the city, the eyewitness and the label each support their venue “unless the other venue holds”. Neither rule outranks the other, so both choices are consistent: one story where the eyewitness prevails (Portonovo), one where the label does (Ancona city).

Reading a line. Each word is one fact of that story: witness(history) — the history page counts as a witness; undercut(events) — the events list is discounted; label_means(city) — this story assumes “Ancona” names the city; venue(portonovo) — this story's answer; at(ancona_municipality) — the meeting was in the comune.

Cautious and brave. Cautious lists what every story agrees on: what the evidence establishes. Brave lists what at least one story contains: what remains possible.

The what-ifs. Adding a fact removes stories. The question is settled when every remaining story has the same venue.

What holds in all of them is what we know: the meeting was within the comune of Ancona, and the events list and the campus claim add no independent weight. What we do not know is the venue. Portonovo is possible under either assumption; Ancona city is possible only if the label names the city and is preferred over the eyewitness. That asymmetry is the honest summary — the evidence leans toward Portonovo — but it is not a proof, and the page keeps both. verified (clingo 5.6.2, 2026-10-20; outputs in the README)

The rules also say what would settle it. Testimony from a 1998 organizer for either venue leaves one answer in every reading — a question for Paolo Atzeni, Luca Cabibbo or Maurizio Panti. Fixing the assumption does less: reading the label as the comune leaves only Portonovo, but reading it as the city still leaves the question open, because the eyewitness disagrees. Corrected before publication: a first version of these rules stated as a known fact that “Ancona” names the comune, while also letting it support the city; a review note caught the hidden assumption, which is now explicit.

For the tour, nothing has changed yet: it keeps 1998 at Ancona's coordinates. Under the Portonovo reading, edition 6 would move about 9 km and become a twin of edition 14, and the optimum would have to be re-solved and re-certified. That has not been done.

Provenance tells you where a number came from, not whether it is true. The lineage graph is the history of this page, mistakes included. Part II sets that history aside and asks only what the final claim rests on — and what you would have to check in order to believe it.

Part II Why you can believe it — evidence

What the claim rests on, which parts are checked, and which you must simply trust.

The evidence (E) — what you actually have to believe

Part I traced how each artifact came about. The tree below asks a different question: which of those artifacts does the claim depend on? Read it bottom-up. The yellow leaves are what you must take on trust; above them sit data files you can open and checks you can re-run; the pink root is the claim. Nodes with a thick border are clickable and open the file they name.

The tree's central distinction is between found and trusted. The 2-opt search found the tour; HiGHS found the proofs, first the cutting-plane run and then the 226-leaf certificate. All of them appear only as gray, dashed helpers. Nothing above them depends on their being correct, because two short Prolog checkers re-verify what they produced, and since 2026-10-16 a second checker, written in Python by another AI agent, re-verifies the lower bound on its own. That is why the trust base is small: seven leaves, most of them about arithmetic and checker code.

Some things are deliberately not in the support structure. The ChatGPT bundle corroborates the result but supports nothing; remove it and the claim still stands. The AI-recalled coordinates appear only as what OpenStreetMap corrected: the one leaf that failed, and the reason for the coordinate audit. And most of Part I is absent altogether. The lineage graph records how things happened; the tree records what the claim depends on. The evidence would stand even if the history were misreported.

The sections below take the tree apart: the two green claims (the sandwich), the blue checks (the trust table), the certificate branch (a proof with no solver in it), and the yellow leaves (known gaps).

Why believe "optimal" without trying every tour?

A fair objection: nobody enumerated the alternatives. With endpoints fixed, the 32 intermediate editions can be ordered in 32! ≈ 2.6 × 1035 ways. At a billion tours checked per second, exhaustive search would take about 8 × 1018 years — some 600 million times the age of the universe. Exhaustion is not merely slow; it is not a thing that happens. verified (arithmetic you can redo on a napkin)

The proof works differently — it is a sandwich, not a search. Two independent facts are produced:

  • An upper bound: a concrete valid tour of length 3,527 km exists (check it: sum its legs — verify.pl does exactly this).
  • A lower bound: no tour — seen or unseen — can be shorter than 3,527 km.

The lower bound comes from relaxation: drop the requirement "the edges form one connected tour" and demand only "every stop touches exactly 2 edges." (A path has two endpoints of degree 1, so exact_tsp.py first closes ours into a cycle with a zero-cost dummy stop joined to editions 1 and 34 — degree 2 then forces both free edges in, and the cheapest cycle is exactly the cheapest path.) Every real tour satisfies these weaker constraints, so the minimum over this larger set can only be ≤ the best tour — a valid bound. The first relaxation yields 2,881 km, but its "solution" is six disconnected mini-loops. So a subtour-elimination cut is added — a linear inequality saying "at least 2 edges must cross this mini-loop's boundary," which is self-evidently true of every genuine tour, so the bound stays valid — and the relaxation is re-solved. Six iterations (2881 → 3273 → 3439 → 3475 → 3477 → 3527) replace 1035 evaluations; at 3,527 the relaxation's own solution is a single tour, so lower = upper = optimum. This is the Dantzig–Fulkerson–Johnson method (1954), the engine inside the record-holding Concorde TSP solver. verified

And a footnote the proof itself supplies: the optimal value is unique, the optimal tour is not. An enumeration run on 2026-08-01 re-solved with a no-good cut forbidding each optimum already found, until the solver's best answer exceeded 3,527 (it did so at 3,529). It reported 16 optimal tours: two routes, each written 8 ways. Note the status of that count: it was reported, but the enumeration script is not in the published bundle and nothing here certifies it. The certificate proves the optimal value, not the number of tours that attain it.

Two kinds of “the same tour”

The 16 are not 16 of one kind. They are 2 × 8, and the two factors mean quite different things.

The ×8 is a symmetry of the data. Rimini (editions 2 and 18), Venice (9, 20), and Villasimius (28, 32) each hosted twice, at identical coordinates, so each pair is 0 km apart and the best tour visits them back to back. Writing Venice as “9, 20” or “20, 9” is the same trip with the labels swapped; three pairs give 2³ = 8 ways to write any tour. This holds under any metric, any rounding, any coordinates: it comes from our choice to key the tour by edition rather than by city, and it is checkable in one line per pair (distance(2,18,0), distance(9,20,0), distance(28,32,0) in distance.pl).

The ×2 is a coincidence of rounding. The second route differs only at the very end, visiting Portonovo before Ancona instead of after. Ancona and Portonovo are 9 km apart, and with distances rounded to whole kilometers the two endings tie exactly. Without the rounding they do not:

EndingRounded legs (km)Rounded totalUnrounded total
Rimini → Ancona → Portonovo → Senigallia (published)93 + 9 + 35137136.16 km
Rimini → Portonovo → Ancona → Senigallia101 + 9 + 27137136.54 km

So in the unrounded geometry there is one optimal route, written 8 ways; the second route ties only in the rounded model, which is the model the certificate is about. Nudge a coordinate or round differently and the tie breaks. verified (the arithmetic, 2026-10-20, from the coordinates in sebd.pl and the projection in trip.py)

What the evidence covers, and what it does not: the certificate proves that no tour is shorter than 3,527 km, and verify.pl checks one tour of exactly that length. That each of the 16 is 3,527 km, and that the twin swaps never change a length, would be easy to add as checks; nobody has. That there are no other optimal tours would need a different certificate — the same kind of case tree, but proving that every tour outside the 16 is at least 3,528 km. Added 2026-10-20: until then this page described both factors in one breath, as “two genuinely different routes, each in 8 twin-relabellings”, and said the detour “cancels exactly” without saying that only the rounding makes it cancel.

Which is why, if you run exact_tsp.py yourself, its last line prints a visiting order that is not the one published above: it is the other route, the one that ends Portonovo → Ancona. Both are among the 16. The solver reports whichever optimum it reaches first; the route file records the one the heuristic found. Nothing is wrong — but it is exactly the kind of discrepancy that should be stated in advance rather than discovered by a suspicious reader.

A proof with no solver in it

"Trust the certificate, not the solver" was, until 2026-08-04, an aspiration on this page: the tour was checkable by hand, but optimality still went through HiGHS. That residual is now closed. In evidence/certificate/ there is a proof object that needs no solver, no linear algebra, no floating point — only integer arithmetic:

cd evidence/certificate
swipl -q -g run -t halt check_certificate.pl

Actual output on 2026-08-04 (macOS, SWI-Prolog 9.0.4, 0.6 s):

PASS (1) 563 edges = 561 published distances + 2 dummy edges
PASS (2) node 35 joins only stops 1 and 34, at zero cost
PASS (3) all 34 cuts are proper subsets (2 =< |S| =< 33)
PASS (4) the 226 leaves form a complete binary tree -- exhaustive
PASS (5) all 226 leaves bound every tour above 3526 km, so >= 3527 km
PASS (6) the published route is a valid 1..34 tour of 3527 km

VERIFIED: no tour of the 34 SEBD editions is shorter than 3527 km,
and a tour of exactly 3527 km exists.  3527 km is optimal.

The whole thing rests on one inequality. Give every stop a number yv (any numbers) and every subtour cut a weight zS ≥ 0 (any weights), and give each edge a reduced cost

d_e  =  c_e - y_u - y_v - (sum of z_S over the cuts that edge e crosses)

Then every tour costs at least

2*(sum of all y_v)  +  2*(sum of all z_S)  +  (sum of min(0, d_e) over all edges)

Why: every stop has exactly two tour edges, every tour crosses every cut at least twice, and a 0/1 variable can only hurt you where de is negative. Three lines of algebra — and, crucially, no assumption about where the numbers came from. A certificate that would break if its numbers came from a liar is not a certificate. verified (the inequality) — but see a second opinion: the checker did not enforce the inequality's preconditions until 2026-10-16.

One such inequality is not enough, and the shortfall is worth knowing: the best bound any single page of potentials can give — 35 numbers and 7 cuts, the full Held–Karp relaxation — is 3,447 km, some 80 km short. So the proof becomes a case analysis: a binary tree splitting on "this edge is used / is not used," where each branch is closed by its own inequality. Ours has 226 leaves, maximum depth 16, a pool of 34 cuts, about 16,000 integers, 99 KB. verified

That size is the finding, and it sharpens the talk's thesis rather than softening it: a napkin proves the tour exists; a small book proves that nothing beats it; and checking the small book still takes 0.6 seconds against 8 × 1018 years of exhaustive search. Note what check (4) does — it verifies the case analysis has no hole. A missing leaf is not an error in a proof; it is an unexamined case, which is exactly the failure a plausible-looking certificate would hide.

What is still trusted, stated plainly: SWI-Prolog's integer arithmetic; the checker itself (short enough to read, but written by the same agent that wrote the generator — and, as the next section shows, short enough to read is not the same as correct); and, as ever, the 561 distances, which are a claim about a model, not about Italy.

A second opinion — the checker had a hole

On 2026-10-16 another AI agent, OpenAI Codex, reviewed the abstract and this page without being told what we already knew. It read the checker, found what it does not check, and built a forgery that exploits it: give every leaf of the certificate a 36th “potential” for a node that does not exist, and claim the chronological route of 15,209 km. The bound counts the extra number; no edge ever subtracts it. The 2026-08-04 checker printed: verified (reproduced here)

VERIFIED: no tour of the 34 SEBD editions is shorter than 15209 km,
and a tour of exactly 15209 km exists.  15209 km is optimal.

The inequality was never wrong; the checker failed to enforce its preconditions (exactly 35 potentials, a model fixed in advance) and loaded the certificate as program code rather than data. verify.pl had a smaller gap of the same kind: it summed the stored legs without checking that they follow the visiting order. Six well-chosen tamperings, all rejected, had not been enough to show that the checker bites — a familiar lesson about tests written by the author of the code.

What survived, and why we know: the 3,527 km result stands. The unchanged certificate passes the hardened checker, and also a second checker Codex wrote for the review — Python, standard library, integer arithmetic, a different author in a different language, now shipped as independent_check.py. That is exactly the step the gaps ledger had listed as “the next honest step” since August. verified

What changed (2026-10-16): the checker now reads the certificate as data, fixes the model's size itself, and runs a new check (0) on the shape of every fact before anything else; verify.pl computes the length from the visiting order and checks each stored leg against it. The forgery and four related ones are now regression tests that must fail (tests/run_tests.py; run with --original to watch the August checker accept three of them). Every changed file, with hashes before and after, is logged in certificate/CORRECTIONS.org and Claude-artifacts/CORRECTIONS.org; the originals are kept beside them.

The same review also caught four errors in this page's prose: a table that gave the post-audit length as if it had been reported on the morning of the talk, a sentence that had the direction of the coordinate error backwards, a spreadsheet cross-check pointing at the other toolchain's distance table, and an overstated rounding bound. All four are corrected above and marked where they were. And it qualified one claim rather than correcting it: the count of 16 co-optimal tours is reported, not certified.

So the second opinion did what the page says second witnesses are for. The first time (the coordinates, 2026-08-01) a disagreeing source caught bad data; this time a disagreeing reader caught a bad checker. In both cases the certified claim survived, and in both cases only because someone other than its author looked.

Appendix: a side experiment from the ASP world

One cross-check deserves a footnote: a generic clingo (answer-set programming) encoding of the tour problem (tsp.lp) reached only 6,709 km after 90 seconds — corroborating, never competing. That feebleness is a known encoding issue, not an ASP one: Bertagnon & Gavanelli (Univ. Ferrara) give new TSP encodings in clingo[DL] — constraint ASP over difference logic — that are orders of magnitude faster, with a further geometric speedup for exactly our case, Euclidean instances with known coordinates (J. Logic and Computation 36(2), 2026, open access). primary source (the paper's claim).

In this page's spirit, we ran the experiment rather than just citing it (2026-07-18, same laptop; files in evidence/DL-experiment/) — and report an honest negative: our adaptation of the paper's SDLT and DLEB encodings (sdlt.lp, dleb.lp, transcribed from Listings 1+5+6 and 1+7+8) returned UNKNOWN in every 90-second run — unable even to confirm a tour of 3,541 km exists, where naive clingo at least reached 7,592 km. (Those two runs used the pre-correction distance table; the corrected instance re-runs naive clingo to 6,709 km, but clingo-dl is no longer installed on this machine, so the SDLT/DLEB runs have not been repeated and their numbers are quoted as of 2026-07-18. stale) This does not refute the paper; it measures the gap between citing a method and reproducing one. Known differences: our clingo-dl 1.5 build lacks the --minimize-variable option their tooling uses (we substituted an outer binary search on the bound); our instance is a path-TSP closed by a zero-cost dummy node; and we implemented none of their geometric (convex-hull) enhancements. Any of these may be decisive — finding out is a conversation to have with the authors, which seems fitting for a memento.
