The SEBD Grand Tour 🍾

The SEBD grand tour, 1993–2026 — with provenance and a certificate.
From Bertram Ludäscher & two AI toolchains, the morning of the SEBD'26 closing talk.

AI prose Thirty-four editions ago, SEBD — founded by Domenico Saccà — began in Gizzeria Lido; this year it reached Senigallia, where the closing talk was about trustworthy AI results: answers that arrive with evidence, not just confidence. The morning of the talk, as a small self-referential demo, we asked: what is the shortest grand tour visiting every SEBD edition, from the first to the thirty-fourth? This page is the keepsake version of that demo — and, in the spirit of the talk, it does not ask you to believe it. Every number below carries the means to check it. Let it make you smile — and let you verify.

primary source The 34 editions and their host cities come from the DBLP index of SEBD proceedings (2026 Senigallia supplied by hand — DBLP doesn't have the future yet). Their coordinates come from OpenStreetMap/Nominatim, checked place by place on 2026-08-01 — see the coordinate audit for why that sentence used to read differently, and what it cost.

The page has two parts. Part I is the provenance: how the answer came about, from DBLP through two AI toolchains to a post-talk audit that found four wrong coordinates. Part II is the evidence: a tree of what the final claim actually rests on, and how to check each piece yourself. The two are related but not the same, and the difference is the point of the page.

The question (Q)

Visit all 34 SEBD editions exactly once, starting at edition 1 (Gizzeria Lido, 1993) and ending at edition 34 (Senigallia, 2026), minimizing total distance. Cities that hosted twice — Rimini, Venice, Villasimius — count once per edition, which is why everything downstream is keyed by edition number, not city name. modeling choice

The answer (A)

AI-generated, verified The optimal tour is 3,527 km — versus 15,209 km in chronological order (76.8% shorter; time travel is inefficient travel). The visiting order, by edition number:

1 29 21 25 24 15 26 19 11 16 12 32 28 33 22 3 23 8
27 10 4 30 17 7 13 5 31 9 20 2 18 6 14 34

modeling choice Honesty about the metric: distances are straight lines on an equirectangular km-plane (reference parallel 42°N, R = 6371 km), rounded to whole kilometres — not roads, not rail, and pointedly not ferries, though the Sicily and Sardinia editions cross open water. Because that projection is a genuine plane embedding, the underlying real-valued metric satisfies the triangle inequality exactly. The rounded matrix we actually ship inherits it only to within the rounding: 22 of the 35,904 ordered triples violate it, every one of them by a single kilometre. Nothing downstream cares — the certificate below is a claim about the shipped integer matrix, whatever its geometry — but a page about checkable claims should not round a number and keep the adjective. Should you actually take the trip, budget extra for the sea legs.

Part I How it came about — provenance

Who computed what, from which sources, and what went wrong along the way.

Two toolchains, three numbers

On the morning of the talk, "the" tour length was reported three ways:

Who said itLengthStatus

The two AI toolchains found essentially the same tour — the orders differ only by swapping the twin editions held at identical coordinates (Venice 9↔20, Rimini 2↔18), which sit at distance zero from each other. Better still, and neither toolchain claimed it at the time: we later proved that GPT's tour is also optimal, for GPT's own coordinates under GPT's own formula. Both narrators were right about their own world. verified

So why two numbers? The honest decomposition is more interesting than the one this page carried until 2026-08-01, which blamed "pure arithmetic" — floating point versus integer rounding, and earth radius 6371.0088 versus 6371. Measured on the identical visiting order, that story was simply false: the radius accounts for 5 metres. Here is the real ledger:

Almost all of it is one documented modeling choice, which is the outcome you want. But note what the old page reported: a gap of only 5.9 km, and it was smaller for a bad reason. Our coordinates were then wrong enough to pull the tour 12.3 km shorter, which happened to cancel most of the 20 km projection difference. The two narrators looked like they nearly agreed; in truth two errors were quietly offsetting. Correcting the data made the disagreement larger and, for the first time, fully explained. verified (the arithmetic; see the coordinate audit)

What turned "almost" into "provably" was neither narrator — it was the certificate (Part II): an exact solver whose lower bound climbs 2881 → 3273 → 3439 → 3475 → 3477 → 3527, meeting a feasible tour exactly there. Lower = upper = optimum.

The model didn't make this trustworthy. The workflow did — and the workflow only caught the data error because a second, disagreeing witness was kept in the room.

The coordinate audit — where this page was wrong

Until 2026-08-01 this page claimed its coordinates were "town centroids, accurate to a couple of km," and invited you to spot-check them against any map. Nobody had. When someone finally did — geocoding all 34 stops against OpenStreetMap/Nominatim — four were wrong, one of them badly:

The errors are not random, which is the interesting part. All four stops are coastal frazioni — a Lido, a Marina, a beach hamlet — and in every case the coordinate had drifted inland toward the parent comune that shares the name. That is exactly the failure mode you would predict of a language model recalling a place from its name rather than looking it up, and exactly what a gazetteer catches in one pass. The other 30 stops came back within 1.5 km. primary source

What it cost: the certified optimum was 3,541 km and is now 3,527 km; the chronological comparison moved 15,245 → 15,209 km. What it did not cost: the visiting order is unchanged, and every structural claim on this page — the sandwich argument, the six cuts, the 16 co-optima — survived verbatim. A certificate proves the tour is shortest for the numbers it was given. It has nothing whatever to say about whether those numbers describe Italy. That is not a flaw in the method; it is the precise boundary of what the method claims, and this page spent two weeks demonstrating it by accident.

The GPT bundle, for its part, had Torre Canne and Squillace Lido right all along, and Castellaneta Marina wrong by 10.0 km and Gizzeria Lido by 3.5 km — so neither toolchain was trustworthy on coordinates, and neither was checkable without a third source. verified

Provenance tells you where a number came from, not whether it is true. The lineage graph is the history of this page, mistakes included. Part II sets that history aside and asks only what the final claim rests on — and what you would have to check in order to believe it.

Part II Why you can believe it — evidence

What the claim rests on, which parts are checked, and which you must simply trust.

The evidence (E) — what you actually have to believe

Part I traced how each artifact came about. The tree below asks a different question: which of those artifacts does the claim depend on? Read it bottom-up. The yellow leaves are what you must take on trust; above them sit data files you can open and checks you can re-run; the pink root is the claim. Nodes with a thick border are clickable and open the file they name.

The tree's central distinction is between found and trusted. The 2-opt search found the tour; HiGHS found the proofs, first the cutting-plane run and then the 226-leaf certificate. All of them appear only as grey, dashed helpers. Nothing above them depends on their being correct, because two short Prolog checkers re-verify what they produced. That is why the trust base is small: seven leaves, two of them about Prolog.

Some things are deliberately not in the support structure. The ChatGPT bundle corroborates the result but supports nothing; remove it and the claim still stands. The AI-recalled coordinates appear only as what OpenStreetMap corrected: the one leaf that failed, and the reason for the coordinate audit. And most of Part I is absent altogether. The lineage graph records how things happened; the tree records what the claim depends on. The evidence would stand even if the history were misreported.

The sections below take the tree apart: the two green claims (the sandwich), the blue checks (the trust table), the certificate branch (a proof with no solver in it), and the yellow leaves (known gaps).

Why believe "optimal" without trying every tour?

A fair objection: nobody enumerated the alternatives. With endpoints fixed, the 32 intermediate editions can be ordered in 32! ≈ 2.6 × 1035 ways. At a billion tours checked per second, exhaustive search would take about 8 × 1018 years — some 600 million times the age of the universe. Exhaustion is not merely slow; it is not a thing that happens. verified (arithmetic you can redo on a napkin)

The proof works differently — it is a sandwich, not a search. Two independent facts are produced:

  • An upper bound: a concrete valid tour of length 3,527 km exists (check it: sum its legs — verify.pl does exactly this).
  • A lower bound: no tour — seen or unseen — can be shorter than 3,527 km.

The lower bound comes from relaxation: drop the requirement "the edges form one connected tour" and demand only "every stop touches exactly 2 edges." (A path has two endpoints of degree 1, so exact_tsp.py first closes ours into a cycle with a zero-cost dummy stop joined to editions 1 and 34 — degree 2 then forces both free edges in, and the cheapest cycle is exactly the cheapest path.) Every real tour satisfies these weaker constraints, so the minimum over this larger set can only be ≤ the best tour — a valid bound. The first relaxation yields 2,881 km, but its "solution" is six disconnected mini-loops. So a subtour-elimination cut is added — a linear inequality saying "at least 2 edges must cross this mini-loop's boundary," which is self-evidently true of every genuine tour, so the bound stays valid — and the relaxation is re-solved. Six iterations (2881 → 3273 → 3439 → 3475 → 3477 → 3527) replace 1035 evaluations; at 3,527 the relaxation's own solution is a single tour, so lower = upper = optimum. This is the Dantzig–Fulkerson–Johnson method (1954), the engine inside the record-holding Concorde TSP solver. verified

And a footnote the proof itself supplies: the optimal value is unique, the optimal tour is not. Rimini (editions 2, 18), Venice (9, 20), and Villasimius (28, 32) each repeat at identical coordinates, so swapping any twin pair changes nothing — 2³ = 8 relabellings of any optimal tour. This page used to stop there and admit it had not checked for others. It has now been enumerated, which took about a minute: re-solve with a no-good cut forbidding each optimum already found, until the solver's best answer exceeds 3,527. It does so at 3,529, and the complete list is exactly 16 optimal tours — two genuinely different routes, each in 8 twin-relabellings. The second route differs only at the very end, arriving Rimini → Portonovo → Ancona → Senigallia instead of Rimini → Ancona → Portonovo → Senigallia; Ancona and Portonovo are 9 km apart and the detour cancels exactly. verified

Which is why, if you run exact_tsp.py yourself, its last line prints a visiting order that is not the one published above. Both are in the 16. The solver reports whichever optimum it reaches first; the route file records the one the heuristic found. Nothing is wrong — but it is exactly the kind of discrepancy that should be stated in advance rather than discovered by a suspicious reader.

"But how do I trust the code?"

The right question — and the answer is that the workflow is arranged so you have to trust remarkably little of it — the blue nodes of the tree. Sorted from easiest to hardest:

To believe…You need to trust…How to shrink that trust
the tour is valid and 3,527 kmverify.pl — 22 lines of PrologRead it (it only sums distance/3 facts and checks a permutation), or ignore it and sum the 561-row CSV in a spreadsheet.

(c) was the genuine residual until 2026-08-04, when it was removed: the lower bound now ships as a solver-free certificate that 161 lines of Prolog check in 0.6 s, using integer arithmetic and nothing else.

That is the asymmetry the talk turns on: the solver may be a thousand lines of heuristics and floating point — irrelevant, because what it emits is a witness whose checking burden is tiny. How the tour was found (2-opt? simulated annealing? divine inspiration?) never enters the argument. Trust the certificate, not the solver.

A proof with no solver in it

"Trust the certificate, not the solver" was, until 2026-08-04, an aspiration on this page: the tour was checkable by hand, but optimality still went through HiGHS. That residual is now closed. In evidence/certificate/ there is a proof object that needs no solver, no linear algebra, no floating point — only integer arithmetic:

cd evidence/certificate
swipl -q -g run -t halt check_certificate.pl

Actual output on 2026-08-04 (macOS, SWI-Prolog 9.0.4, 0.6 s):

PASS (1) 563 edges = 561 published distances + 2 dummy edges
PASS (2) node 35 joins only stops 1 and 34, at zero cost
PASS (3) all 34 cuts are proper subsets (2 =< |S| =< 33)
PASS (4) the 226 leaves form a complete binary tree -- exhaustive
PASS (5) all 226 leaves bound every tour above 3526 km, so >= 3527 km
PASS (6) the published route is a valid 1..34 tour of 3527 km

VERIFIED: no tour of the 34 SEBD editions is shorter than 3527 km,
and a tour of exactly 3527 km exists.  3527 km is optimal.

The whole thing rests on one inequality. Give every stop a number yv (any numbers) and every subtour cut a weight zS ≥ 0 (any weights), and give each edge a reduced cost

d_e  =  c_e - y_u - y_v - (sum of z_S over the cuts that edge e crosses)

Then every tour costs at least

2*(sum of all y_v)  +  2*(sum of all z_S)  +  (sum of min(0, d_e) over all edges)

Why: every stop has exactly two tour edges, every tour crosses every cut at least twice, and a 0/1 variable can only hurt you where de is negative. Three lines of algebra — and, crucially, no assumption about where the numbers came from. A certificate that would break if its numbers came from a liar is not a certificate. verified

One such inequality is not enough, and the shortfall is worth knowing: the best bound any single page of potentials can give — 35 numbers and 7 cuts, the full Held–Karp relaxation — is 3,447 km, some 80 km short. So the proof becomes a case analysis: a binary tree splitting on "this edge is used / is not used," where each branch is closed by its own inequality. Ours has 226 leaves, maximum depth 16, a pool of 34 cuts, about 16,000 integers, 99 KB. verified

That size is the finding, and it sharpens the talk's thesis rather than softening it: a napkin proves the tour exists; a small book proves that nothing beats it; and checking the small book still takes 0.6 seconds against 8 × 1018 years of exhaustive search. Note what check (4) does — it verifies the case analysis has no hole. A missing leaf is not an error in a proof; it is an unexamined case, which is exactly the failure a plausible-looking certificate would hide.

And to answer the obvious suspicion — does the checker actually bite, or does it pass everything? Six tampered copies were run against it:

TamperingResponse
delete one leafFAIL (4) the case analysis has a hole
zero out one leaf's potentialsFAIL (5) a leaf does not close
make a cut multiplier negativeFAIL (5) a leaf does not close
claim 3,528 km insteadFAIL (5) a leaf does not close
falsify one edge cost (645 → 545 km)FAIL (1) an edge disagrees with distance.pl
enlarge a cut to the whole node setFAIL (3) a cut set is not a proper subset

One tampering is accepted, and should be: nudge a potential by a few units and you still have a valid proof, just a different one. A checker that rejected that would be policing provenance, not validity. verified (all seven runs; see README.org)

What is still trusted, stated plainly: SWI-Prolog's integer arithmetic; the 161-line checker itself (short enough to read, but written by the same agent that wrote the generator — a second checker in another language, by another author, is the next honest step); and, as ever, the 561 distances, which are a claim about a model, not about Italy.

Legend — what kind of claim is this?

primary source — fact from an external primary source (DBLP), checkable there.

modeling choice — a documented approximation (centroid coordinates, straight-line metric); not wrong, but a choice you should know about.

AI-generated, verified — produced by an AI, then independently checked by a program whose output is quoted verbatim above.

AI prose, unverified — narrative flavor. Charming, we hope; evidence, it is not.

These badges label the epistemic status of a sentence. The colours in the evidence tree label something else: a node's role in the argument — assumption, data, check, claim, or helper.

Appendix: a side experiment from the ASP world

One cross-check deserves a footnote: a generic clingo (answer-set programming) encoding of the tour problem (tsp.lp) reached only 6,709 km after 90 seconds — corroborating, never competing. That feebleness is a known encoding issue, not an ASP one: Bertagnon & Gavanelli (Univ. Ferrara) give new TSP encodings in clingo[DL] — constraint ASP over difference logic — that are orders of magnitude faster, with a further geometric speedup for exactly our case, Euclidean instances with known coordinates (J. Logic and Computation 36(2), 2026, open access). primary source (the paper's claim).

In this page's spirit, we ran the experiment rather than just citing it (2026-07-18, same laptop; files in evidence/DL-experiment/) — and report an honest negative: our adaptation of the paper's SDLT and DLEB encodings (sdlt.lp, dleb.lp, transcribed from Listings 1+5+6 and 1+7+8) returned UNKNOWN in every 90-second run — unable even to confirm a tour of 3,541 km exists, where naive clingo at least reached 7,592 km. (Those two runs used the pre-correction distance table; the corrected instance re-runs naive clingo to 6,709 km, but clingo-dl is no longer installed on this machine, so the SDLT/DLEB runs have not been repeated and their numbers are quoted as of 2026-07-18. stale) This does not refute the paper; it measures the gap between citing a method and reproducing one. Known differences: our clingo-dl 1.5 build lacks the --minimize-variable option their tooling uses (we substituted an outer binary search on the bound); our instance is a path-TSP closed by a zero-cost dummy node; and we implemented none of their geometric (convex-hull) enhancements. Any of these may be decisive — finding out is a conversation to have with the authors, which seems fitting for a memento.
